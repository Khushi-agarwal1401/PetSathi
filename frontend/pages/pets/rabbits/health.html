Know What's Normal

Signs of a Healthy Rabbit

Rabbits are creatures of routine. Learning what is normal for your rabbit—how much they eat, drink, move, and produce droppings—helps you notice small changes before they become serious.

Bright and Clear Eyes

Eyes should be bright, clear, and free from discharge, with no squinting, redness, or swelling.

Clean Ears and Nose

Ears should be clean and free from discharge, while the nose should be dry without unusual mucus or sounds.

Healthy Coat and Skin

A glossy coat and healthy skin with no bald patches, flakes, sores, or constant scratching.

Normal Appetite and Drinking Habits

Eating hay, greens, and pellets steadily, and drinking fresh water every day.

Regular, Healthy Droppings

Plenty of firm, round droppings produced consistently is one of the best signs of good gut health.

Comfortable Movement and Activity

Hopping, stretching, exploring, and binkying comfortably without stiffness, limping, or reluctance to move.

Know the Risks

Common Rabbit Health Problems

Rabbits can be affected by several common health issues. Learn the early signs to watch for and contact a rabbit-experienced veterinarian if you are concerned. This page does not diagnose or treat illness.

Dental Problems

Rabbit teeth grow continuously, so overgrowth, misalignment, or painful spurs can affect eating and grooming.

Signs to watch for
  • Drooling or a wet chin
  • Difficulty chewing or dropping food
  • Reduced appetite or weight loss

Digestive Problems

Sensitive digestive systems can be upset by sudden diet changes, low fibre, stress, or infection.

Signs to watch for
  • Soft, watery, or misshapen droppings
  • Reduced appetite or drinking
  • Bloating or a painful-looking abdomen

Gastrointestinal Stasis

The gut slows or stops moving, which can become a life-threatening emergency very quickly.

Signs to watch for
  • No droppings or much fewer than usual
  • Not eating or refusing favourite foods
  • Hunched posture, lethargy, or pain

Respiratory Problems

Infections or irritants can affect the nose, throat, and lungs, making breathing uncomfortable.

Signs to watch for
  • Sneezing or nasal discharge
  • Noisy, rapid, or laboured breathing
  • Eye discharge or reduced activity

Ear Problems

Ear infections, mites, or abscesses can cause discomfort and may affect balance.

Signs to watch for
  • Head shaking or scratching at the ears
  • Discharge, redness, or a bad smell
  • Head tilt or loss of balance

Skin and Parasite Problems

Parasites, allergies, or skin infections can irritate the coat and make your rabbit uncomfortable.

Signs to watch for
  • Scratching, biting, or rubbing the skin
  • Hair loss, scabs, dandruff, or redness
  • Visible fleas, mites, or tiny specks

Sore Hocks and Foot Problems

Pressure, poor flooring, or excess weight can damage the skin on the feet, especially the hocks.

Signs to watch for
  • Sores, bald patches, or redness on the feet
  • Swelling or crusting around the hocks
  • Limping or reluctance to move
Act Early

Warning Signs & Symptoms

Rabbits are prey animals and often hide illness. Contact a rabbit-experienced veterinarian promptly if you notice any of these changes.

Loss of Appetite

Not eating, eating much less, or refusing favourite foods is always a reason to seek advice.

Reduced or Absent Droppings

Fewer droppings, smaller droppings, or none at all can signal a digestive emergency.

Unusual Droppings

Soft, watery, misshapen, or mucus-covered droppings should be discussed with a veterinarian.

Unusual Tiredness or Hiding

Lethargy, reluctance to move, or hiding more than usual can be an early sign of pain or illness.

Difficulty Breathing

Laboured, noisy, or rapid breathing, or breathing with the mouth open, needs urgent veterinary care.

Head Tilt or Balance Problems

A tilted head, circling, rolling, or unsteady movement should be assessed promptly.

Unusual Eye or Nose Discharge

Discharge, crusting, redness, or swelling around the eyes or nose can indicate infection.

Sudden Changes in Behaviour

New aggression, fear, withdrawal, or a sudden change in routine can be a sign of discomfort.

Rabbits hide signs of illness

As prey animals, rabbits may appear normal until they are seriously unwell. Do not wait for symptoms to become severe. If your rabbit stops eating, stops passing droppings, has difficulty breathing, or shows a sudden change in behaviour, contact a rabbit-experienced veterinarian immediately—day or night.

Teeth That Keep Growing

Dental Health

Rabbit teeth grow continuously throughout life. A high-fibre diet, daily observation, and regular veterinary checks help keep them worn and comfortable.

Teeth Continue to Grow

Rabbit incisors and cheek teeth grow continuously and need regular wear to stay healthy.

High-Fibre Hay Supports Wear

Unlimited grass hay encourages chewing and helps teeth wear naturally.

Monitor Eating and Chewing

Watch for slower eating, dropping food, or choosing soft foods over hay.

Watch for Dental Warning Signs

Drooling, difficulty eating, weight loss, a wet chin, or bulging eyes need veterinary assessment.

Arrange Veterinary Dental Checks

Ask your veterinarian to examine your rabbit's teeth during routine checkups.

Never trim your rabbit's teeth at home

Dental problems are painful and can worsen quickly. Do not attempt to trim, file, or treat your rabbit's teeth yourself. Seek help from a rabbit-experienced veterinarian.

Gut Health Matters

Digestive Health

A rabbit's digestive system needs constant movement. Fibre, fresh water, and a steady routine are the foundation of good gut health.

Importance of a High-Fibre Diet

Grass hay should make up most of the diet and be available at all times.

Daily Monitoring of Food Intake

Check that your rabbit is eating hay, greens, and pellets every day.

Monitoring Droppings

Note the number, size, and consistency of droppings each day.

Access to Fresh Water

Provide clean, fresh water daily in a bowl or bottle.

Avoiding Sudden Diet Changes

Introduce new foods gradually to avoid upsetting the gut.

Recognising Possible Digestive Emergencies

No droppings, not eating, a painful abdomen, or diarrhoea need urgent veterinary help.

Stops eating or passing droppings? Act now.

A rabbit that stops eating or passing droppings can deteriorate very quickly. Contact a rabbit-experienced veterinarian immediately. Do not wait overnight, and do not give human medicines or home remedies unless your vet instructs you to.

Stay Ahead

Preventive Health Care

Routine care helps detect problems early and keeps your rabbit comfortable throughout life.

Regular Veterinary Checkups

Schedule routine wellness examinations with a veterinarian experienced in rabbit medicine.

Vaccinations as Advised by a Veterinarian

Vaccination recommendations vary by region. Ask your veterinarian what is appropriate for your rabbit.

Dental Checks

Have your rabbit's teeth checked regularly, especially if eating or chewing habits change.

Weight Monitoring

Track weight over time. Unexpected loss or gain should be discussed with your veterinarian.

Grooming and Skin Checks

Brush regularly and check the coat, skin, ears, eyes, and feet for changes.

Hygiene and Parasite Prevention

Keep the living area clean and ask your veterinarian about safe parasite prevention.

Daily Behaviour Monitoring

Watch for changes in appetite, activity, droppings, and interaction with family members.

Know When to Seek Help

When to Visit a Vet

Some health changes can wait for a routine appointment, while others need urgent care. Use these guidelines and always follow your veterinarian's advice.

Routine Checkups

Regular visits help build a health baseline and catch small changes early.

  • Regular wellness examinations
  • Dental and weight checks
  • Vaccination discussions

Noticeable Changes

Book an appointment if you notice a change from your rabbit's normal routine.

  • Appetite changes
  • Unusual droppings
  • Changes in movement or behaviour

Emergency Attention

Contact a rabbit-experienced veterinarian or emergency clinic immediately.

  • Not eating
  • No droppings
  • Breathing difficulties
  • Severe weakness or injury

Emergency care: do not wait

If your rabbit shows any emergency sign, contact a rabbit-experienced veterinarian or emergency clinic immediately. Keep your rabbit warm, calm, and in a quiet carrier while you arrange transport. Do not wait to see if things improve, and do not give human medicines or home remedies unless your vet instructs you to.

Important Veterinary Information

This page is educational and does not replace professional veterinary advice, diagnosis, or treatment. Always consult a rabbit-experienced veterinarian about your rabbit's health. If you suspect an emergency, contact your veterinarian or an emergency animal hospital immediately.

Quick Answers

Rabbit Health FAQs

Clear answers to common questions about keeping your rabbit healthy.

What are the signs of a healthy rabbit?

A healthy rabbit usually has bright, clear eyes, clean ears and nose, a glossy coat, a steady appetite and drinking routine, plenty of regular droppings, and comfortable movement. Learning your rabbit's normal routine is the best way to notice changes early.

What health problems are common in rabbits?

Common concerns include dental problems, digestive problems, gastrointestinal stasis, respiratory infections, ear problems, skin and parasite issues, and sore hocks. Because rabbits hide illness, contact a rabbit-experienced veterinarian if you notice any warning signs.

How can I keep my rabbit's teeth healthy?

Offer unlimited grass hay to support natural tooth wear, monitor eating and chewing habits, and arrange regular veterinary dental checks. Watch for drooling, difficulty eating, a wet chin, or weight changes. Never trim or file your rabbit's teeth at home.

How can I support good digestive health?

Feed a high-fibre diet based on grass hay, provide fresh water daily, monitor food intake and droppings, and introduce new foods gradually. A rabbit that stops eating or passing droppings needs urgent veterinary assessment.

When should I contact a veterinarian?

Contact a rabbit-experienced veterinarian if you notice appetite changes, unusual droppings, tiredness or hiding, breathing difficulties, a head tilt, unusual discharge, or a sudden change in behaviour. Not eating, no droppings, breathing difficulties, and severe weakness or injury are emergencies.
